Research

Accurate psychological classification depends on measurement models that stay reliable when standard assumptions are violated. My work sits at the intersection of psychometrics and machine learning, and asks how methods perform under the conditions researchers face in practice.

Classification under item bias

A central focus of my methodological work is the effect of unmodeled differential item functioning (DIF) on diagnostic accuracy. DIF occurs when people with the same level of a latent trait respond differently to an item because of group membership, which introduces systematic bias that can distort classification, especially for minority group members. Using large Monte Carlo simulations, I compare item response theory (IRT) models with random forest classifiers across many conditions, and find that random forests consistently outperform IRT classifications when DIF cannot be directly modeled. This points to machine learning classifiers as practical alternatives when item bias is suspected but hard to specify. I am extending this work to multidimensional contexts, where the interaction between latent structure and unmodeled DIF is still largely unknown.

  • Bain, C.M., Manapat, P.D., Manapat, D.M., Brennan, K.B., & Grimm, K.J. (2026). When DIF goes unmodeled: assessing the viability of random forest for diagnostic classification. Journal of Behavioral Data Science.

Variable selection with complex and incomplete data

Psychological data are often high dimensional, incomplete, or both. Researchers tend to rely on regularized regression such as LASSO and elastic net, which perform well under ideal conditions but degrade as complexity grows. Through large simulation studies, I evaluate LASSO, elastic net, and genetic-algorithm-based selection under data that are missing completely at random and missing at random. The genetic algorithm proved most robust to changes in completeness, which gives practical guidance for incomplete data. To make these methods usable, I publish reproducible tutorials in R.

Measuring and characterizing misophonia

Much of my applied work focuses on misophonia. Despite a published consensus definition, there are no formal diagnostic criteria, which complicates recruitment, clinical translation, and the development of reliable scales and biomarkers. Using factor analysis and multidimensional IRT, I led the first comprehensive psychometric evaluation of the Duke Misophonia Questionnaire, identifying a five-factor structure and mapping where the instrument measures most precisely. Building on that, I co-lead work on a short form using IRT and machine learning item reduction, and I lead a latent profile analysis to identify misophonia subtypes that may reflect distinct neurophysiological mechanisms.

Collaborations across domains

I contribute quantitative expertise to applied teams across occupational and industrial-organizational psychology, health behavior and cessation research, and educational psychology. This work typically means assessing dimensionality with factor analysis, running item-level IRT, and evaluating validity and reliability. I also work at the intersection of psychology and computational science, applying agent-based modeling and simulation to emergent social phenomena.
